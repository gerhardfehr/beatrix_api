% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit impressive linguistic fluency, yet persistent concerns remain regarding their epistemic reliability. Empirical studies show that models often overstate uncertain claims, fail to maintain stable epistemic calibration across contexts, and provide predictions or recommendations without explicit justification. While existing work largely attributes these failures to hallucinations, miscalibration, or insufficient prompting, we argue that such explanations remain incomplete.

In this paper, we present a structural framework that specifies necessary conditions for epistemically responsible generative systems. Central to our approach is \emph{Empirical Stability of Language} (ESL), an empirical property capturing whether the strength of a linguistic claim is proportionate to the available evidence and remains stable across contextual variations. We show that ESL is necessary but not sufficient for epistemic reliability.

We further demonstrate that predictive and intervention-oriented statements require explicit domain models that externalize assumptions and enable counterfactual reasoning. This requirement is particularly critical in domains involving human behavior, where outcomes are causally mediated by decisions and not governed by stable laws. We argue that no amount of prompting, scale, or training can substitute for these structural components.

Our contribution is neither architectural nor algorithmic. Instead, we provide a formal necessity argument establishing that epistemic reliability is a system property, not a model property. By separating linguistic generation, empirical epistemic calibration, and theoretical inference, our framework clarifies why fluent language alone cannot guarantee responsible generative behavior and identifies the minimal structural components required for epistemically legitimate AI systems.
\end{abstract}


% ============================================================================
% 1. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================================
\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved unprecedented fluency and coverage across domains. Yet despite their apparent competence, persistent concerns remain regarding epistemic reliability: models frequently overstate uncertain claims, blur distinctions between established knowledge and speculation, and provide recommendations without transparent justification.

A growing body of work diagnoses these failures in terms of hallucination, miscalibration, or overconfidence. In this paper, we argue that such diagnoses, while correct, are incomplete. The core problem is not merely that language models sometimes err, but that epistemic reliability cannot be guaranteed by linguistic competence alone.

We introduce a structural framework that specifies what components must exist for epistemically responsible generative behavior to be possible in principle. Our contribution is not architectural or algorithmic. Instead, we identify necessary conditions for epistemic legitimacy and show why prompting, scale, or training improvements cannot substitute for them.


% ============================================================================
% 2. BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
% ============================================================================
\section{Background and Motivation}

\subsection{Empirical Stability of Language (ESL)}

Recent empirical work has shown that LLM outputs vary substantially in epistemic calibration across prompts, contexts, and task formulations. To analyze this phenomenon, we build on the concept of \emph{Empirical Stability of Language} (ESL).

ESL refers to the empirically observable property that the strength of a linguistic claim does not exceed the strength of the available evidence, and that this calibration remains stable across contextual variations. Formally, ESL follows the principle:
\[
K(c) \le M(c)
\]
where $K(c)$ denotes claim strength and $M(c)$ denotes evidence strength.

Crucially, ESL is descriptive and diagnostic. It does not authorize or prohibit statements; it measures whether language remains epistemically calibrated.

\subsection{The Limits of Empirical Calibration}

While ESL captures an essential dimension of epistemic responsibility, it alone cannot explain why models fail differently across tasks, nor why correct calibration in descriptive contexts often collapses in predictive or intervention-oriented settings.

This observation motivates a deeper structural analysis.


% ============================================================================
% 3. THE ACTIVATION BINDING PROBLEM
% ============================================================================
\section{The Activation Binding Problem ($\alpha$)}

Empirical studies indicate that many LLMs possess substantial latent domain knowledge, yet fail to apply this knowledge reliably during generation. We denote this phenomenon as the \emph{Activation Binding Problem}, captured by the coefficient $\alpha$.

Intuitively, $\alpha$ measures the probability that available knowledge is activated and applied during generative tasks. A low $\alpha$ indicates that knowledge exists but is not consistently used.

Importantly, improving $\alpha$ through prompting or instruction tuning does not guarantee epistemic reliability. Activation alone does not determine whether a claim is justified, nor whether an inference is valid.


% ============================================================================
% 4. WHY PROMPTING CANNOT GUARANTEE BINDING
% ============================================================================
\section{Why Prompting Cannot Guarantee Binding}

A natural response to epistemic failures is to improve prompting. However, prompting operates entirely within the linguistic system and remains subject to interpretation.

We formalize this limitation as follows:

\begin{proposition}[Prompt Insufficiency]
There exists no prompt $\pi$ such that epistemic binding is guaranteed across contexts.
\end{proposition}

Prompts may encourage caution, uncertainty marking, or self-reflection, but they cannot enforce invariant behavior. Prompt interpretation itself is model-dependent and unstable.

Thus, prompting may improve ESL or $\alpha$ empirically, but it cannot serve as a structural guarantee.


% ============================================================================
% 5. EXTERNALIZING EPISTEMIC CONTROL
% ============================================================================
\section{Externalizing Epistemic Control}

To achieve epistemic reliability, key inferential steps must be externalized from the language model.

We distinguish between three functions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linguistic generation} (LLM output)
    \item \textbf{Epistemic calibration} (ESL)
    \item \textbf{Inference and intervention} (domain models)
\end{enumerate}

Only the first is performed by the LLM itself. The latter two require explicit structure.


% ============================================================================
% 6. THE THREE-LEVEL SEPARATION PRINCIPLE
% ============================================================================
\section{The Three-Level Separation Principle}

We propose the \emph{Three-Level Separation Principle}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Level} & \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
Linguistic & Generation & Produce language \\
Epistemic & Calibration (ESL) & Ensure $K \le M$ \\
Theoretical & Domain models & Enable inference \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This separation clarifies why fluent language cannot substitute for theory, and why epistemic reliability is a system property, not a model property.


% ============================================================================
% 7. ABSTRACT FORMAL MODELS
% ============================================================================
\section{Abstract Formal Models}

\subsection{ESL as an Empirical Standard}

ESL defines a \emph{standard}, not a model. Any approach that operationalizes claim strength and evidence strength and evaluates their alignment qualifies as ESL-compliant.

Consequently, competition arises at the level of ESL models, not at the level of the ESL definition itself.

\subsection{Domain Models for Prediction and Intervention}

For predictive and intervention tasks, explicit domain models are required. A domain model specifies assumptions, variables, and derivation rules.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Incorrect models are allowed. Implicit models are not.}
\end{quote}

This distinction is essential: epistemic responsibility requires explicit derivation, not empirical correctness.


% ============================================================================
% 8. HUMAN BEHAVIOR AS A MODEL-OBLIGATORY DOMAIN
% ============================================================================
\section{Human Behavior as a Model-Obligatory Domain}

Among all domains in which generative systems are deployed, human behavior occupies a special structural role. This is not a normative claim, but a consequence of two objective properties.

First, human behavior is \emph{causally mediating}: in many real-world settings, outcomes depend directly on how humans perceive, decide, and act in response to information, recommendations, or interventions. Second, human behavior is \emph{law-underdetermined}: unlike physical or formal systems, it is not governed by stable, universal laws that uniquely determine responses.

As a result, whenever a predictive or intervention statement concerns human behavior, explicit behavioral models become obligatory. Without such models, claims about expected outcomes, policy effects, or decision impacts lack epistemic grounding.

Importantly, this does not imply that behavioral models must be correct, complete, or optimal. It only implies that some explicit model must exist. Wrong models are epistemically preferable to implicit ones, because their assumptions can be inspected, challenged, and revised.


% ============================================================================
% 9. IMPLICATIONS
% ============================================================================
\section{Implications}

\subsection{Epistemic Reliability as a System Property}

A central implication of our framework is that epistemic reliability is not a property of language models, but of the systems in which they are embedded. No amount of scale, training data, or prompt engineering can substitute for explicit epistemic calibration and theoretical modeling.

This perspective reframes current debates about hallucinations and overconfidence. These phenomena are not merely model failures; they are structural consequences of systems that ask language to perform inferential work it cannot justify.

\subsection{Why Model Quality Becomes the Primary Axis of Differentiation}

Once linguistic generation and epistemic calibration are standardized, application-level differentiation necessarily shifts to the quality of the explicit domain models used for prediction and intervention.

In other words, language ceases to be the competitive bottleneck. Instead, competition arises around:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the adequacy of theoretical assumptions,
    \item the transparency of derivations,
    \item and the robustness of domain-specific models.
\end{itemize}

This shift is a structural consequence of the framework, not an empirical performance claim.

\subsection{Competition Under a Fixed Epistemic Standard}

Because ESL defines a criterion rather than a method, multiple competing implementations are possible. Different systems may operationalize claim strength, evidence strength, and contextual stability in distinct ways, while still satisfying the formal definition of ESL.

This enables methodological competition under a shared epistemic standard, analogous to competition among statistical models, risk assessments, or scientific theories.


% ============================================================================
% 10. LIMITATIONS AND SCOPE
% ============================================================================
\section{Limitations and Scope}

This work is intentionally limited in scope.

First, we do not formalize a concrete ESL implementation. ESL is introduced as an empirical standard, not as a specific algorithm.

Second, we do not guarantee the correctness of any domain model. The framework permits wrong models; it disallows unmodeled inference.

Third, we do not propose an architecture or system design. Questions of integration, deployment, and optimization are explicitly deferred.

These limitations are not omissions, but boundary conditions. The goal of this paper is to establish necessary structural requirements, not sufficient conditions for correctness.


% ============================================================================
% 11. CONCLUSION
% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We have argued that epistemic reliability in generative AI systems cannot be achieved through prompting, scale, or linguistic competence alone. Instead, it requires a structural separation between language generation, empirical epistemic calibration, and explicit theoretical modeling.

Empirical Stability of Language (ESL) provides a diagnostic standard for calibrated language use. However, ESL alone is insufficient for prediction and intervention. Whenever systems make claims about future outcomes or recommend actions---especially in domains involving human behavior---explicit domain models are indispensable.

Our central claim is therefore structural:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Reliable generative systems require binding language to explicit models, not persuading models to behave reliably.}
\end{quote}

This framework does not prescribe how to build such systems, nor does it guarantee correctness. It specifies what must be present for epistemic responsibility to be possible at all.

In subsequent work, we demonstrate how these abstract principles can be instantiated in concrete AI systems.

\begin{quote}
\textit{Language can describe the world, but only explicit models can justify inference---and only systems that bind the two can be epistemically reliable.}
\end{quote}
