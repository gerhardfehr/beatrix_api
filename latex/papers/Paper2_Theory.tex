% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generating fluent, contextually appropriate text across a wide range of domains. As a result, they are increasingly deployed in settings that involve not only descriptive summarization but also predictive and prescriptive claims about the world. However, these deployments expose a fundamental structural problem: LLMs routinely make inferential and intervention-relevant statements without explicit models of the domains they describe, and without formal constraints on the epistemic force of their language.

This paper develops a minimal theoretical framework that distinguishes three classes of tasks---description, prediction, and intervention---and shows that each class imposes different modeling requirements. We argue that while descriptive tasks require epistemic control over language, predictive and intervention-oriented tasks require explicit theory-based domain models. Human behavior occupies a special role among such domains, as it is causally decisive in many real-world settings while being governed by no fixed laws.

We further argue that epistemic reliability is a system-level property, not a property of language models alone. We introduce the concept of \emph{Empirical Stability of Language} (ESL)---the property that linguistic claim strength does not exceed evidence strength---as a diagnostic criterion for epistemically calibrated systems. Our contribution is structural rather than algorithmic: we do not propose architectures or training methods, but identify necessary conditions for epistemically responsible generative systems.
\end{abstract}


% ============================================================================
% 1. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================================
\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models are rapidly transitioning from tools for text generation to systems that participate in decision-making contexts. They summarize research, explain phenomena, forecast outcomes, and increasingly recommend actions. This expansion raises a central question: under what conditions are the claims produced by such systems epistemically legitimate?

Much of the current discourse focuses on improving model accuracy, alignment, or training data. While important, these approaches implicitly assume that epistemic reliability is a property of the language model itself. In this paper, we argue that this assumption is false. Reliability does not arise from better language modeling alone, but from how language generation is embedded within a system that explicitly represents domain assumptions and constrains what may be asserted.

We propose a minimal framework that distinguishes (i) \emph{what is being said}, (ii) \emph{what the statement is about}, and (iii) \emph{whether the claim strength is proportionate to evidence}. This leads to the concept of \emph{Empirical Stability of Language} (ESL) as a diagnostic measure, alongside the functional separation between language models and domain models.

\subsection{Scope and Contribution}

This paper is purely theoretical. We do not propose architectures, training methods, or performance benchmarks. Instead, we:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Distinguish three task classes: description, prediction, and intervention.
    \item Show that each class imposes different modeling requirements.
    \item Argue that human behavior requires explicit modeling when it mediates outcomes.
    \item Establish that epistemic reliability is a system-level property.
    \item Introduce the principle of separating linguistic generation from domain modeling and epistemic control.
\end{enumerate}

Subsequent work will demonstrate how these abstract principles can be instantiated in concrete systems.


% ============================================================================
% 1.2 RELATED WORK
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Related Work}

Our framework builds on several emerging research streams while making a distinct contribution.

\paragraph{Epistemic Risk in Generative AI.}
Recent work has begun to characterize the epistemic risks inherent in generative systems. Research on epistemic injustice in AI highlights how models can amplify misinformation and produce epistemic inequities by misleading users about what is known versus what is merely plausible \citep{epistemicrisk2025}. Studies of plausibility as a failure mode show that LLM errors arise not only from incorrect facts but from how model plausibility interacts with human interpretation \citep{plausibilityfailure2025}. These findings support our core claim that fluency does not imply reliability, and that explicit controls on assertion are necessary.

\paragraph{AI and Epistemic Authority.}
A growing literature examines how generative AI alters traditional epistemic roles. Users often treat AI outputs as authoritative without adequate critical grounding \citep{epistemicauthority2025}. Normative frameworks have begun to examine the conditions under which epistemic dependence on AI is justified \citep{epistemiclegitimacy2025}. This research motivates our argument that epistemic licensing cannot be left to implicit model behavior---systems must explicitly govern how claims are made.

\paragraph{Epistemology of Generative AI.}
Philosophical work situates generative models within epistemological frameworks, examining how they construct and validate claims \citep{automatingepistemology2025}. These analyses emphasize the sociotechnical nature of truth claims and the structural distinction between linguistic fluency and epistemic validity. Our framework operationalizes these insights by proposing concrete separations between generation, derivation, and licensing.

\paragraph{What Current Work Does Not Provide.}
Existing research characterizes epistemic risks but does not formalize solutions. There is no prior work that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Distinguishes task classes (description, prediction, intervention) by their modeling requirements
    \item Defines Empirical Stability of Language (ESL) as a diagnostic property ($K \le M$)
    \item Argues systematically for the separation of domain models from linguistic models
    \item Identifies human behavior as a special modeling domain requiring explicit theory
\end{itemize}

Our contribution is therefore novel: we provide not merely a diagnosis of epistemic unreliability, but a structural framework specifying what components are necessary for reliable generative systems.


% ============================================================================
% 2. DESCRIPTION, PREDICTION, AND INTERVENTION
% ============================================================================
\section{Description, Prediction, and Intervention}

To clarify the modeling requirements of generative systems, we distinguish three task categories.

\subsection{Descriptive Tasks}

Descriptive tasks answer questions such as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What is known about phenomenon $X$?
    \item How is concept $Y$ defined in the literature?
    \item What evidence exists for effect $Z$?
\end{itemize}

These tasks do not require the system to derive new facts about the world. Instead, they involve organizing, summarizing, and presenting existing knowledge. Crucially, however, even description involves \emph{assertive language}: statements are framed as established, uncertain, or unknown.

Thus, description is not epistemically neutral. Without explicit constraints, a system may overstate consensus, conflate concepts with labels, or blur the boundary between evidence and speculation.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Requirement:} Descriptive tasks require \emph{Empirical Stability of Language}---the property that claim strength does not exceed evidence strength---to be satisfied and verifiable.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Predictive Tasks}

Predictive tasks answer questions of the form:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What will happen if $X$ occurs?
    \item How is outcome $Y$ expected to change under condition $Z$?
\end{itemize}

Prediction introduces counterfactual reasoning. It asserts that certain outcomes follow from certain conditions. This necessarily presupposes a causal or structural relationship in the underlying domain.

Language alone cannot justify such relationships. Plausible narratives are not derivations.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Requirement:} Predictive tasks require:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An explicit \emph{domain model} that supports counterfactual inference.
    \item Verification that claims satisfy ESL ($K \le M$), ensuring prediction strength matches evidence.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Intervention Tasks}

Intervention tasks go further. They answer questions such as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What should we do to achieve outcome $Y$?
    \item Which policy or action is preferable?
\end{itemize}

Formally, intervention combines prediction with choice and normativity. It compares multiple possible actions under explicit or implicit objectives.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Requirement:} Intervention tasks require:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item One or more \emph{domain models} to evaluate consequences.
    \item An explicit \emph{objective or value function}.
    \item \emph{Epistemic speech control}, because recommendations carry heightened authority and risk.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}


% ============================================================================
% 3. DOMAIN MODELS AND THEIR NECESSITY
% ============================================================================
\section{Domain Models and Their Necessity}

A \emph{domain model} is an explicit, theory-based structure that derives outcomes from assumptions. Importantly, we do not argue for any particular model, only for the necessity of having one whenever inference is required.

\subsection{When Domain Models Are Not Required}

Domain models are not required when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The task is purely descriptive.
    \item No counterfactual or prognostic claims are made.
    \item The domain is governed by formal or physical laws and no human decisions are causally involved.
\end{itemize}

Examples include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Formal mathematics and logic
    \item Program semantics
    \item Classical physics of closed systems
    \item Chemical reactions under controlled laboratory conditions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When Domain Models Are Required}

Domain models are required whenever outcomes depend on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choices
    \item Reactions
    \item Adaptation
    \item Strategic behavior
    \item Information or incentives
\end{itemize}

In such cases, description alone is insufficient. Something must explain \emph{why} certain outcomes follow.


% ============================================================================
% 4. HUMAN BEHAVIOR AS A SPECIAL DOMAIN
% ============================================================================
\section{Human Behavior as a Special Domain}

Human behavior plays a distinctive role in this framework.

\subsection{Causal Centrality}

In many real-world domains---medicine, law, safety, economics, public policy---the ultimate effectiveness of interventions depends on how people behave. Compliance, adherence, interpretation, and strategic response determine outcomes.

\subsection{Absence of Laws}

Unlike physics or logic, human behavior is not governed by fixed laws. It is context-sensitive, information-dependent, and often strategic. As a result, it cannot be reliably inferred from linguistic plausibility.

\subsection{Error Amplification}

Small errors in behavioral assumptions can produce large real-world consequences. Overconfidence in behaviorally mediated predictions is therefore particularly dangerous.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Conclusion:} Human behavior is rarely sufficient to explain outcomes, but it is almost never irrelevant. Where it is causally implicated, it must be explicitly modeled.
\end{quote}


% ============================================================================
% 5. WHY LANGUAGE MODELS ARE NOT BEHAVIORAL MODELS
% ============================================================================
\section{Why Language Models Are Not Behavioral Models}

A common objection is that LLMs implicitly encode human behavior, having been trained on human-generated data. This is true descriptively but false structurally.

Language models approximate distributions over text, not decision processes. They lack:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Explicit assumptions
    \item Causal structure
    \item Counterfactual invariance
    \item Falsifiability
\end{itemize}

They can imitate how people \emph{talk about} behavior, but they cannot justify claims about how people \emph{will act}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Implicit representations are epistemically insufficient where accountability is required.}
\end{quote}


% ============================================================================
% 6. EMPIRICAL STABILITY OF LANGUAGE (ESL)
% ============================================================================
\section{Empirical Stability of Language}

Across all task categories, one property is universally required: epistemic calibration of language.

\subsection{Formal Definition}

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of all assertive statements $c$. For each statement $c \in \mathcal{C}$, define:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K(c) \in [0,1]$: \textbf{Claim Strength}---the linguistic force of the assertion (certainty, generality, causality, implication)
    \item $M(c) \in [0,1]$: \textbf{Evidence Strength}---the empirical basis supporting the claim (replication, effect stability, methodology)
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[ESL Principle]
A statement $c$ satisfies \emph{Empirical Stability of Language} if and only if:
\[
K(c) \le M(c)
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[ESL Property]
A set of statements $\mathcal{C}$ possesses Empirical Stability of Language if and only if:
\[
\forall c \in \mathcal{C}: K(c) \le M(c)
\]
\end{definition}

\subsection{Canonical Definition}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Empirical Stability of Language (ESL)} is the empirically observable property of assertive language whereby the strength of a linguistic claim is proportionate to the strength of its supporting evidence, and this proportionality remains stable across variations in context, prompt formulation, and task framing.
\end{quote}

\subsection{What ESL Is and Is Not}

ESL is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \emph{descriptive} property---it characterizes language use
    \item A \emph{diagnostic} criterion---it identifies epistemic calibration
    \item An \emph{empirically measurable} condition---it can be tested
\end{itemize}

ESL is \emph{not}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An optimization function
    \item A decision rule
    \item A normative enforcement mechanism
    \item A control layer or gating system
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
\emph{ESL is not a rule about what may be said, but a measure of how responsibly language is used.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Universality of ESL}

Because every generated statement carries epistemic force, ESL applies universally---including in domains that do not require explicit domain models. ESL measures whether systems remain epistemically calibrated; it does not generate, authorize, or prohibit content.


% ============================================================================
% 7. KNOWLEDGE ACTIVATION AND MODEL VARIABILITY
% ============================================================================
\section{Knowledge Activation and Model Variability}

LLMs differ significantly in their ability to activate latent knowledge during generation. We denote this empirical property as $\alpha$, the \emph{knowledge activation} or \emph{binding coefficient}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha$ varies across models
    \item $\alpha$ is influenced by training and prompting
    \item $\alpha$ is not under explicit control
\end{itemize}

The relationship between $\alpha$ and ESL is complementary but distinct:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha$ \emph{explains} why knowledge may fail to surface
    \item ESL \emph{measures} whether claims exceed their evidential basis
\end{itemize}

A system may have high $\alpha$ (good knowledge activation) yet fail ESL (overclaiming). Conversely, a system may have low $\alpha$ yet satisfy ESL by appropriately hedging its claims.

\begin{quote}
\emph{ESL measures, $\alpha$ explains, domain models derive.}
\end{quote}


% ============================================================================
% 8. SYSTEM-LEVEL SEPARATION PRINCIPLE
% ============================================================================
\section{The System-Level Separation Principle}

Our analysis leads to a clear separation of responsibilities:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Function} \\
\midrule
Language models & Generate text \\
Domain models & Derive outcomes \\
ESL criterion & Measure calibration ($K \le M$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Epistemic reliability is a property of the system, not of any single component.}
\end{quote}

This principle has immediate consequences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Improving language models alone cannot guarantee reliability.
    \item Prompting cannot substitute for explicit modeling.
    \item Wrong models are acceptable; unmodeled claims are not.
    \item Behavior must be modeled when it mediates outcomes.
    \item ESL calibration is universally required.
\end{itemize}


% ============================================================================
% 9. CENTRAL HYPOTHESES
% ============================================================================
\section{Central Hypotheses}

This work rests on two complementary hypotheses:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Behavioral Derivation):} Statements about human behavior must be derived from explicit formal models, not generated narratively.
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2 (Epistemic Permission):} Even correctly derived statements may only be made if their epistemic preconditions are satisfied.
\end{quote}

H1 is operationalized through explicit domain models (e.g., Behavioral Change Models).\\
H2 is diagnosed through the ESL criterion ($K \le M$).

Neither hypothesis alone is sufficient:
\begin{itemize}
    \item H1 without H2 yields correct but epistemically overreaching predictions
    \item H2 without H1 yields epistemically cautious but behaviorally unfounded claims
\end{itemize}

Together, they define the minimal requirements for reliable behavioral AI.

% 10. WHY CURRENT APPROACHES DO NOT SUFFICE
% ============================================================================
\section{Why Current Approaches Do Not Suffice}

The market pursues several strategies for improving LLM reliability:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Better training (RLHF, alignment)
    \item Better prompting (chain-of-thought, instructions)
    \item Retrieval and grounding (RAG)
    \item Guardrails and filters
    \item Personas and simulation
\end{itemize}

All share a common assumption: \textbf{the LLM itself should be improved or persuaded}. They attempt to make the model more reliable through optimization, instruction, or constraint.

Our framework draws a different conclusion:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The LLM is not the appropriate locus for epistemic or behavioral authority.}
\end{quote}

We propose decoupling rather than optimization. Binding rather than persuasion. This is not an incremental improvement but a paradigm shift---which is precisely why it has been rarely pursued.

\subsection{Why This Conclusion Is Rarely Drawn}

The conclusion is technically feasible but structurally unattractive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It does not produce state-of-the-art benchmarks
    \item It reduces model autonomy
    \item It demystifies ``intelligence''
    \item It requires explicit theory
\end{itemize}

In short: it ends a mode of thinking rather than extending it. This is not a dead end but a terminus---one that becomes relevant only when probabilistic approaches no longer suffice.

\section{Limitations and Scope}

\subsection{What This Paper Does Not Do}

This paper is deliberately limited. We do not:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Propose a specific architecture or system
    \item Provide implementation details
    \item Make performance claims or comparisons
    \item Offer training procedures or fine-tuning methods
    \item Promise scalability or generalization
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What This Paper Does}

This paper sets conceptual boundaries. We:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Distinguish description, prediction, and intervention
    \item Show when domain models are required
    \item Establish human behavior as a special modeling domain
    \item Prove that LLMs are not behavioral models
    \item Define ESL as a universal diagnostic criterion ($K \le M$)
    \item Derive the system-level separation principle
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope of Claims}

Our claims are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural}: We identify necessary components, not sufficient implementations.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical}: We establish what is required for reliability, not how to achieve it.
    \item \textbf{Normative}: We argue how systems \emph{should} be conceptualized, not how they currently work.
\end{itemize}

% 12. CONCLUSION
% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Generative AI systems increasingly move beyond description into prediction and intervention. This transition exposes a fundamental gap between linguistic fluency and epistemic legitimacy. We have argued that this gap cannot be closed by better language modeling alone.

Reliable AI systems must address three distinct requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Description}: Accurate representation of what is known
    \item \textbf{Derivation}: Explicit models for what follows (prediction, intervention)
    \item \textbf{Calibration}: Claim strength proportionate to evidence (ESL: $K \le M$)
\end{enumerate}

Human behavior occupies a special position in this framework, as it is both causally decisive and inherently model-dependent. Where behavior mediates outcomes, explicit behavioral models are indispensable.

Ultimately, language models should speak---but not decide what is true, what follows, or what ought to be done. Those responsibilities belong to explicit domain models and systems that satisfy the ESL criterion.

\begin{quote}
\textit{We describe with language, predict with models, intervene with responsibility---and license all of it epistemically.}
\end{quote}

\vspace{1em}

In subsequent work, we demonstrate how these abstract principles can be instantiated in a concrete AI system.
