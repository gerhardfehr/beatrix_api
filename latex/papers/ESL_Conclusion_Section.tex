\section{Conclusion}

This work argues that reliable language model behavior depends not only on what a model knows, but on whether it can activate that knowledge during generation. By formally distinguishing latent knowledge from knowledge activation, we identify activation competence ($\alpha$) as an independent and critical dimension of LLM performance.

Through the Activation Challenge, we show that even under fully formalized and epistemically restrictive prompts, some models systematically fail to apply knowledge they demonstrably possess. This finding challenges the common assumption that hallucination and epistemic unreliability primarily stem from missing information or insufficient prompt design. Instead, our results suggest that a substantial portion of such failures arise from limitations in knowledge activation itself.

Importantly, we find that explicit prompting and epistemic constraints have bounded effectiveness. While they can reduce interpretational ambiguity, they do not guarantee correct knowledge application. This indicates that improvements in epistemic reliability may require changes at the level of training objectives, model architecture, or internal control mechanisms, rather than relying solely on inference-time interventions.

By providing a formal vocabulary, a controlled diagnostic framework, and clear falsification criteria, this work lays the foundation for a more principled study of knowledge activation in generative models. We hope that future research will build on this framework to better understand, measure, and ultimately improve how language models use what they already know.

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Understanding what language models know is not enough; understanding when they can use that knowledge is the next critical challenge.}
\end{quote}
